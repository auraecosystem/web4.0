## 1. Gender Roles: Historical Evolution & Modern Shifts
Gender roles dictate the behaviors, tasks, and responsibilities socially assigned to individuals based on their perceived sex.

* Pre-Industrial Societies: Roles were largely organized around survival. While often simplified as "men hunt, women gather," labor was interdependent. Women typically managed domestic tasks and child-rearing compatible with nursing, while men handled high-risk or long-distance tasks.
* The Industrial Revolution: The shift to factory work created the public sphere (associated with men and wage-earning) and the private sphere (associated with women, unpaid domestic labor, and emotional care). This institutionalised the "breadwinner/homemaker" model.
* Modern Dynamics: The 20th and 21st centuries saw a massive influx of women into the formal workforce, driven by economic necessity and feminist movements. However, modern sociology frequently highlights the "second shift"—where women work full-time jobs but still perform a disproportionate share of unpaid domestic labor. Today, roles are increasingly fluid, focusing on dismantling rigid binaries and advancing workplace equity.

## 2. Economic Roles: Labor, Capital, and the State
Economic structures organize how a society produces, distributes, and consumes goods and services.

* Labor (Workers): Sell their time, physical energy, and intellectual skills in exchange for wages. Labor is the engine of production and the primary source of consumer demand.
* Capital (Owners/Investors): Own the means of production—such as factories, technology, land, and financial assets. Their role is to allocate resources, take financial risks, and generate profit.
* Consumers: Purchase goods and services. Their preferences and spending power drive market demand and dictate what businesses produce.
* The State (Regulator): Intervenes to correct market failures, enforce contracts, protect worker rights, collect taxes, and provide public goods (like roads and education) that private capital cannot or will not fund alone.

## 3. Sociological Perspectives on Social Roles
Sociologists use distinct theoretical frameworks to explain why roles exist and how they affect individuals.

+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

|                        SOCIOLOGICAL PERSPECTIVES                        |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

| 1. FUNCTIONALISM                                                        |
|    • View: Roles keep society stable and running smoothly.              |
|    • Key Concept: The division of labor ensures efficiency.             |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

| 2. CONFLICT THEORY                                                      |
|    • View: Roles maintain inequality and benefit the powerful.          |
|    • Key Concept: Explains exploitation (e.g., Capital vs. Labor).      |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+

| 3. SYMBOLIC INTERACTIONISM                                              |
|    • View: Roles are fluid and created through daily interactions.       |
|    • Key Concept: "Role-taking" (acting out expectations dynamically).   |
+-------------------------------------------------------------------------+


* Functionalism (Emile Durkheim, Talcott Parsons): Sees society as a complex system whose parts work together to promote solidarity and stability. Roles exist because they serve a purpose. For example, traditional gender roles were viewed by early functionalists as an efficient division of labor (instrumental roles for men, expressive roles for women) that kept the family unit stable.
* Conflict Theory (Karl Marx, Max Weber): Views society as a site of inequality and struggle for resources. Roles are not neutral; they are imposed by the dominant class to maintain power. For instance, the role of the "worker" is structurally exploited by the owner of "capital," and traditional gender roles are seen as a way to subjugate women to unpaid labor that benefits the capitalist economy.
* Symbolic Interactionism (George Herbert Mead, Erving Goffman): Takes a micro-level approach, arguing that roles are not rigid scripts we passively follow. Instead, roles are constantly negotiated, performed, and modified through daily human interaction. Goffman’s dramaturgical analysis suggests we are all actors on a stage, using "impression management" to play our specific roles convincingly to our audience.
