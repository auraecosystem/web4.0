Key Concepts & Macro-Economic StructuresTo build a compelling sociological analysis, your essay must utilize precise academic terminology to describe economic structures. [1] (https://openoregon.pressbooks.pub/socgender/chapter/1-3-sociology-of-gender/)Gender Division of Labor: The social practice of assigning different work tasks and societal responsibilities to individuals based on their perceived gender roles. It encompasses divisions within the home (unpaid labor) and divisions in the formal market (paid employment). [1] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/373273572_Division_of_Labor_Gender), [2] (https://archive.unescwa.org/gender-division-labour), [3] (https://www.researchgate.net/publication/313541611_Gender_Roles_and_Division_of_Labour)New Home Economics (Comparative Advantage): Formulated by economist Gary Becker, this model assumes nuclear households function as utility-maximizing units. It argues that if women hold a biological or structural comparative advantage in household production and men excel in market production, total family utility is maximized when individuals specialize along those lines. Sociologists critique this for ignoring restrictive cultural norms and power imbalances. Real-World Economic Disparities & StatisticsWhen writing an essay, grounding arguments in empirical, macro-level statistics from authoritative international organizations is critical for validity. [1] (https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/unpaid-care-work-prevents-708-million-women-participating-labour-market), [2] (https://www.who.int/publications/b/62953)Unpaid Care Work & "The Second Shift"The Burden: Across the global economy, roughly 708 million women are completely excluded from entering the formal labor market due to the structural barriers of unpaid care work and severe time poverty.The Second Shift: Coined by sociologist Arlie Hochschild in her landmark 1989 study, this concept highlights that even when women maintain full-time paid employment, they return home to complete a "second shift" of domestic work, child care, and emotional labor. Hochschild's original time-use metrics demonstrated that women worked an average of 15 hours more per week than men across their combined paid and unpaid tasks. This means that over a calendar year, working women perform the equivalent of an extra month of 24-hour days compared to their male partners. [1] (https://www.ilo.org/resource/news/unpaid-care-work-prevents-708-million-women-participating-labour-market), [2] (https://www.unwomen.org/en/articles/explainer/gender-equality-in-2025-gains-gaps-and-the-342t-choice), [3] (https://vaccination.gov.ng/libweb/NYVDbs/271010/Arlie%20Hochschild%20The%20Second%20Shift.pdf), [4] (https://anniewright.com/the-second-shift-and-the-stalled-revolution-why-youre-so-exhausted/), [5] (https://sk.sagepub.com/ency/edvol/motherhood/chpt/second-shift-third-shift)The Gender Wage Gap & SegregationOECD General Metrics: As of recent tracking, the gender wage gap stands at an average of 11.5% across OECD nations, meaning full-time working women earn approximately 88 cents for every dollar earned by men at median earnings.Sector Segregation: Women comprise roughly 67% of global employment in the health and care sector. Yet, within this exact sector, women wage workers earn approximately 20% less than men—a gap wider than that observed in many other economic sectors.The Glass Ceiling vs. Glass Escalator: The glass ceiling represents invisible structural barriers that prevent qualified women and minority groups from ascending to senior executive echelons. Conversely, the glass escalator describes the hidden structural advantages that accelerate the promotion of men (particularly white men) into leadership roles when they enter female-dominated "pink collar" professions, such as teaching or primary healthcare. Glossary of Key Sociological TermsDoing Gender: A micro-level sociological concept stating that gender is an ongoing routine performance embedded in virtually all social interactions.Glass Ceiling: An unacknowledged, invisible barrier arising from cultural expectations and corporate networks that prevents qualified women and minorities from advancing to peak administrative levels.Glass Escalator: The structural acceleration of men into advanced supervisory positions and higher wages when they enter traditionally female-dominated, pink-collar professions.Horizontal Segregation: The uneven distribution of men and women across entirely different industries or occupational types (e.g., engineering vs. elementary education).Second Shift: The unpaid domestic chores, management, and family care tasks performed by working individuals (disproportionately women) after finishing formal shifts in the paid labor market.Sex vs. Gender: Sex denotes immutable biological and physiological attributes (such as chromosomes and reproductive organs). Gender represents fluid, socially constructed behaviors, expectations, and roles assigned by a given culture based on those sex differences.Vertical Segregation: The clustering of one gender at the lowest levels of an occupational hierarchy, while the opposite gender dominates the highest positions of power and pay within the same industry.
